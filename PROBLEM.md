# Problem Statement

## Why is this a problem?

Medicine packaging is the main source of safety information a person has at the moment they use a medicine: what it is, how strong it is, who made it, which batch it came from, when it expires and how to take it. That information is frequently:

- printed in very small type or in technical language;
- damaged, faded or partly hidden on worn packaging;
- hard to act on for someone who cannot easily read it.

Separately, small pharmacies record incoming stock, batch numbers and expiry dates manually. Manual entry is slow, invites transcription mistakes, and makes it easy to miss batches approaching expiry.

## Who experiences it?

- **Consumers and patients** who need medicine information explained clearly.
- **People with limited literacy or reading difficulty**, including those who struggle with small or technical labels.
- **Small pharmacies and pharmacy workers** who capture stock and expiry data by hand.

Pharmacists, healthcare professionals and official verification bodies are secondary stakeholders where official mechanisms exist.

## Why does it matter now?

Multimodal vision models can now read text from photographs, including awkward angles and small print, and return it as structured data. That makes a photo-first tool practical for the first time: no barcode scanner, no typing, no specialist hardware. It also makes clear explanation and voice output achievable in local languages such as Swahili.

*Supporting statistics on counterfeit or expired medicines and on literacy in Kenya will be added here with cited sources. We deliberately do not include unsourced figures.*

## What are we trying to change?

- People can **understand** what is on their medicine packaging without struggling with the label.
- People can see **evidence-based verification signals** (expiry problems, missing or inconsistent information) and are told plainly when something **could not be verified**.
- Pharmacies can **capture and monitor** stock from a photo with minimal manual entry, and quickly answer "which medicines expire within 60 days?".

## What we are explicitly not trying to do

- Prove from a photograph that a medicine is genuine or counterfeit.
- Prescribe medicines, determine treatment or change a prescribed dosage.

Where the tool cannot be sure, it says so and points the user to a pharmacist or an official source. That uncertainty is a feature, not a failure.
